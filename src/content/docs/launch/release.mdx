## How does the app reach 50 participants?

| Channel | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **TestFlight** (iOS) | closed groups; up to 10,000 external testers | external testers need Beta App Review; **each build expires after 90 days** |
| **Google Play closed testing** | a known list of testers on Android | the same policies apply as for a public app, including the health declaration |
| **Public store listing** | anyone can find and install it | full review; the listing is public, so every word in it counts |

<Predict id="ninety" question="The clinic's study lasts six months, and participants on iPhones get the app through TestFlight. What will happen in month four if you do nothing?">

The build **expires after 90 days**, and the app stops working for the iPhone participants in the middle of the study. A six-month study needs at least one new build, uploaded and approved in time, and a short message to participants. This is the kind of detail that turns into a support crisis if nobody plans it.

</Predict>

## What the stores check

Store review is not only about crashes. For health apps, the text and the data matter as much:

- **Medical claims.** Under Apple's App Store Review Guidelines (1.4.1), a medical app may have to provide documentation of regulatory clearance. Claims your intended purpose does not cover are the fastest way to a rejection.
- **Health declaration.** Google Play asks **every** app for a health-apps declaration in Play Console, including apps without health features.
- **Privacy labels.** Apple's *App Privacy* details and Google Play's *Data safety* section must match what the app really collects. Build them from your data inventory, not from memory.
- **Platform rules.** For example, from 31 August 2026 Google Play requires new apps and updates to target Android 16 (API level 36). Rules like this change every year.

## Versions and builds

Every release has a **version** (what people see, such as `1.3.0`) and a **build number** (which exact build, such as `12`). The About screen from Part 2 shows both, so support can tell which build a participant runs. Keep **release notes** for every build, and roll out gradually where the store allows it (staged rollout on Google Play, phased release on the App Store), so that a bad build reaches only a few people.

```bash title="Release builds (Flutter)"
flutter build appbundle --release --build-name=1.3.0 --build-number=12 \
  --dart-define=APP_VERSION=1.3.0+12 --dart-define=API_BASE_URL=https://…
flutter build ipa --release --build-name=1.3.0 --build-number=12 \
  --dart-define=APP_VERSION=1.3.0+12 --dart-define=API_BASE_URL=https://…
```

Signing keys and keystores never go into the repository. Developer accounts cost money too: at the time of writing, about 99 USD a year for Apple and a one-off 25 USD for Google. Check the current prices before you budget.
